ABSTRACT

A simple question behind a large review problem

Content teams can have more pages to review than they have time to review them. That creates a practical problem: even when a large amount of search and engagement data is available, it can still be difficult to decide where human attention should go first. This project asks whether historical search visibility and engagement signals can help rank content observations by their observed future click-through rate (CTR), giving a reviewer a more consistent place to begin.

The analysis uses anonymized monthly observations from the FlyRank ML Internship dataset. February signals were used as the historical feature window for training, March CTR was used as the future outcome for that training relationship, and March signals were then evaluated against April CTR. This time-aware setup keeps the information used for prediction before the future outcome being measured.

The Random Forest model achieved a Spearman rank correlation of 0.174270 on the final evaluation window, compared with 0.169878 for the Week 4 baseline. The difference was 0.004392, which is small. The result is therefore better understood as a modest observed improvement in ranking performance rather than a dramatic predictive gain.

The final output is a ranked review queue containing 158,549 observations. The queue is intended to organize human review rather than decide automatically what should be changed. Its purpose is deliberately narrow: help answer where a reviewer should look first.

01

INTRODUCTION

The problem is not a lack of data. It is a lack of attention.

Search analytics can produce a large number of observations across content pages and time periods. A reviewer may know how often a page appeared in search, where it appeared on average, how many clicks it received, how many sessions followed, and how much interaction occurred on the page. The difficulty starts after those numbers arrive. When there are thousands of observations, inspecting every page with the same amount of attention is rarely practical.

This makes prioritization useful. Rather than trying to diagnose every page automatically, this project uses machine learning as a way of organizing review work. A higher-ranked observation is not declared broken, and it is not promised to improve after a particular intervention. It simply receives earlier attention because the model places it higher within the observed ranking.

THE DECISION THIS RESEARCH SUPPORTS

Which content observations should we investigate first when review capacity is limited?

That distinction matters. This project does not attempt to explain Google's ranking systems, nor does it try to prove that changing a title, metadata field, piece of content, or engagement signal will cause a specific future result. The goal is much more practical: create a repeatable ranking that can help direct limited human attention.

02

DATA

Three monthly windows linked forward in time

The analysis uses the anonymized FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release hosted on Hugging Face. Daily observations were accessed through DuckDB and then aggregated to the anonymized client-content level for each monthly window. Three consecutive months were used so that the historical information used for prediction could be kept separate from the future outcome used for evaluation.

WINDOW ROLE IN THE STUDY
February 2026 Historical features used for model training
March 2026 Training outcome and evaluation feature window
April 2026 Final future outcome window

The three raw monthly windows contained 2,621,783 observations in February, 3,611,061 in March, and 3,901,060 in April. After monthly aggregation, the resulting feature tables contained 153,559 February client-content observations, 176,738 March observations, and 194,760 April observations.

The model used five historical signals: impressions- weighted average search position, search impressions, search clicks, sessions, and scroll events. Monthly impressions, clicks, sessions, and scroll events were summed, while search position was calculated using an impressions-weighted average. CTR was calculated as clicks divided by impressions.

Rows without available Google Search Console data were excluded from the monthly source windows. The public paper does not expose client names, domains, URLs, private queries, credentials, or raw warehouse exports. Only anonymized identifiers and aggregate research results are shown.

03

METHODOLOGY

Predicting the next window without using the next window

The problem was framed as a supervised ranking task. The aim was not to produce a perfect numerical forecast for every individual observation, but to learn enough structure from historical data to create a useful ordering of future observations.

Historical features

Five features were supplied to the model: average search position, search impressions, search clicks, sessions, and scroll events. All five were measured in the historical feature window. No future outcome information was included as an input.

Prediction target

FUTURE CTR = FUTURE CLICKS / FUTURE IMPRESSIONS

February features were paired with March CTR to form the training relationship. March features were then paired with April CTR for the final evaluation. This creates a clear chronological structure: historical features first, future outcome later.

Model and preprocessing

The final model was a Random Forest Regressor with 100 trees, a maximum depth of 10, a minimum of 10 samples per leaf, and a fixed random state of 42. Missing or infinite feature values were replaced using medians calculated from the training data only. The same training medians were then applied to the evaluation data.

Validation design

TRAINING FEATURES FEBRUARY
→
TRAINING OUTCOME MARCH
→
FINAL EVALUATION APRIL

Leakage checks were built into the workflow. Future CTR was excluded from the feature list, preprocessing statistics came from training data only, and the final evaluation took place on a later monthly outcome window. This does not eliminate every possible source of bias, but it makes the temporal structure of the evaluation explicit.

04

RESULTS

The model performed better, but only by a little.

The final evaluation compares the Random Forest with the Week 4 baseline using the same March-to-April evaluation population. Spearman rank correlation is the main ranking metric because the practical question is whether higher model scores tend to correspond to higher observed future CTR.

WEEK 4 BASELINE 0.169878 Spearman rank correlation
RANDOM FOREST 0.174270 Spearman rank correlation
OBSERVED DIFFERENCE +0.004392 Absolute difference

The Random Forest produced a slightly stronger observed ranking correlation than the baseline. The difference was 0.004392. That is a small improvement, and it would be misleading to describe the model as a highly accurate predictor of individual content performance.

The more useful interpretation is operational. The model provides a directional signal that can help organize a large review queue, especially when a reviewer has limited capacity and needs a consistent starting point.

What happened near the top of the ranking?

A second way to examine the ranking is to look at the mean observed future CTR among the highest-ranked observations. This mirrors a practical review scenario: when only a small number of pages can be inspected, what tends to be present near the top of the queue?

TOP 20 0.065211
TOP 100 0.055425
TOP 500 0.034707

The mean observed future CTR is highest among the first 20 observations and decreases as the ranked group becomes larger. This is consistent with the intended use of the model as a prioritization tool: under a smaller review budget, the top of the queue contains a stronger concentration of the observed future-CTR signal.

These results do not mean that every highly ranked observation is a problem, nor do they guarantee that a future content change would improve performance. They show how the ranking behaved in this particular evaluation window.

05

MODEL INTERPRETATION

Which signals mattered most to the model?

The Random Forest provides an internal measure of how much different input features contributed to prediction decisions across its trees. This gives a useful way to inspect the model, but it needs to be interpreted carefully. A feature can be important to a model without being a controllable cause of the outcome.

Average search position contributed the largest importance value at approximately 0.515, followed by impressions at approximately 0.325 and clicks at approximately 0.156. Sessions and scroll events contributed much less under this model configuration.

IMPORTANT INTERPRETATION

Feature importance describes how the model used its inputs. It does not prove that changing a highly important feature would cause future CTR to change.

RANKED RECOMMENDATIONS

Turning predictions into a practical review queue

The final model output was turned into an action playbook containing 158,549 ranked client-content observations. Each observation receives a rank, directional model score, priority level, supporting reason codes, and a suggested review starting point.

THE ACTION PLAYBOOK ANSWERS

Which observations should a reviewer investigate first?

Priority levels

HIGH
Ranks 1–20

20 observations receiving the earliest review attention.

MEDIUM
Ranks 21–100

80 observations for a broader review pass.

LOW
Ranks 101 and below

158,449 observations for later review when more capacity is available.

The reason codes provide additional context from observed search and engagement signals. The largest groups were VISIBLE_POSITION|HIGH_IMPRESSIONS, VISIBLE_POSITION, GENERAL_REVIEW, and HIGH_IMPRESSIONS. These are descriptive signals intended to help a reviewer understand what to inspect, not causal explanations of why a page behaved a certain way.

The review process

A reviewer can begin with the highest-ranked observations, inspect the supporting reason codes, and then examine the actual page and its search context. The model may point toward search visibility, clicks, impressions, sessions, or scroll activity, but the reviewer decides whether that signal matters in context.

The workflow deliberately stops before automatic action. It does not publish content changes, rewrite metadata, delete pages, alter URLs, change technical controls, or claim that a specific intervention will increase CTR. The output is a prioritization queue for investigation, not an autonomous optimization system.

THE PLAYBOOK ANSWERS “Where should human review begin?” NOT “What exact change should automatically be made?”
07

LIMITATIONS & HONEST FRAMING

What this study cannot tell us

The most important limitation is that the underlying data is observational. The model learns relationships from historical measurements, but those relationships do not prove that changing one of the measured signals will cause a particular future CTR result.

The final evaluation also covers a specific sequence of monthly windows. Search demand, competition, seasonality, query mix, SERP features, external events, and other conditions can change over time. A result observed across the February-to-April 2026 windows should therefore not be assumed to remain identical in another period.

The model also uses an intentionally limited feature set. Important contextual information such as search intent, detailed content quality, business value, editorial constraints, and external events is not fully represented. Human reviewers may know something important about a page that the model cannot see.

Finally, this project does not explain, reverse-engineer, or establish how Google's ranking systems work. The results should be described using careful terms such as observed, measured, directional, and decision-support.
